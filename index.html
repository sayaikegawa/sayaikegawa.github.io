Job Market Paper

Unrealized Capital Gains Tax, Business Investment, and Wealth Inequality

Draft coming soon

Abstract: Unrealized capital gains constitute a large share of wealth at the top of the U.S. wealth distribution and are concentrated in privately held businesses, yet they remain untaxed under the current realization-based capital-gains tax system. This paper studies the welfare consequences of replacing realization-based taxation with an accrual-based (mark-to-market) tax regime, with particular emphasis on entrepreneurial business assets. Using a quantitative general equilibrium model, I find that an economy-wide switch to accrual-based taxation reduces private business production by 8% and aggregate output by 5%, because the efficiency loss from entrepreneurial entry deterrence outweighs the efficiency gains from mitigating capital lock-in. In contrast, applying accrual-based taxation only to wealthy households yields a consumption-equivalent welfare gain of 1%. By targeting wealthy households, who are more likely to be incumbent entrepreneurs, this reform dampens entry deterrence while reducing inequality.

Reassessing Japan’s Fiscal Health

with Ellen McGrattan and Adrian Peralta-Alva

Presented at CEBRA Annual Meeting 2025

Abstract

Japan faces considerable fiscal challenges due to rapid population aging. A large body of literature argues that high consumption tax rates or other significant policy changes will be required to stabilize public economicss and maintain retirement, health, and long-term care systems. However, all existing quantitative studies ignore the role of intangible capital, thereby understating Japan’s true wealth base and overstating the necessary tax burden. This paper re-examines the fiscal issue by incorporating newly compiled data on Japan’s intangible investments into a dynamic overlapping generations model. We find that the consumption tax rate required to finance increased transfers for the elderly is approximately 6 percentage points lower when accounting for intangible capital, as the enhanced capital stock boosts production and broadens the tax base. Neglecting intangible capital thus significantly overstates Japan’s fiscal challenge.

Intangible Investment in Japan: A Comparison with the United States

Presented at the International Monetary Fund, Research Department (2024)

Abstract

GDP measures do not incorporate all intangible investment undertaken by firms, although the importance of intangible assets has been increasingly recognized. To provide a more comprehensive picture of macroeconomic conditions, this paper measures intangible investment in Japan, including additional intangible assets that are currently under discussion for inclusion in national accounts. The findings show that intangible investment in Japan is more than twice as large as reported in current national accounts statistics, and that its ratio to GDP is increasing and exceeds that of the United States. Incorporating these additional intangible assets has significant implications for macroeconomic measurement. First, Japan’s five-year GDP growth rate is estimated to increase by approximately 1 percentage point relative to the current measure, while the corresponding estimate for the United States remains largely unchanged. In addition, the impact on GDP varies substantially across industries. Finally, the estimated labor share decreases by about 4 percentage points in both countries.
