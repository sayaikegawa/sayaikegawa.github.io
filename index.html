Job Market Paper

Unrealized Capital Gains Tax, Business Investment, and Wealth Inequality

  • PDF

Abstract: Unrealized capital gains constitute a large share of wealth at the top of the U.S. wealth distribution and are concentrated in privately held businesses, yet they remain untaxed under the current realization-based capital gains tax system. Motivated by recent proposals to tax unrealized capital gains, this paper studies the welfare consequences of accrual-based (mark-to-market) taxation using a quantitative general equilibrium model of entrepreneurship and business sales. I find that accrual-based taxation applied to all households reduces private business production by 8%, aggregate output by 5%, and consumption-equivalent welfare by 3%, as reduced entrepreneurial entry outweighs the benefits of greater business turnover. In contrast, applying accrual-based taxation only to very wealthy households can reduce inequality and generate welfare gains of up to 1%. Targeting wealthy households, who are more likely to be incumbent business owners, limits entry deterrence, while reduced capital accumulation lowers equilibrium wages and encourages entrepreneurial entry among less wealthy households.

Work in Progress

Reassessing Japan’s Fiscal Health

with Ellen McGrattan and Adrian Peralta-Alva

Presented at CEBRA Annual Meeting (2025)

Draft available upon request

Abstract

Japan faces considerable fiscal challenges due to rapid population aging. A large body of literature argues that high consumption tax rates or other significant policy changes will be required to stabilize public finances and maintain retirement, health, and long-term care systems. However, all existing quantitative studies ignore the role of intangible capital, thereby understating Japan’s true wealth base and overstating the necessary tax burden. This paper re-examines the fiscal issue by incorporating newly compiled data on Japan’s intangible investments into a dynamic overlapping generations model. We find that the consumption tax rate required to finance increased transfers for the elderly is approximately 6 percentage points lower when accounting for intangible capital, as the enhanced capital stock boosts production and broadens the tax base. Neglecting intangible capital thus significantly overstates Japan’s fiscal challenge.

Intangible Investment in Japan: A Comparison with the United States

Presented at the IMF Research Department (2024)

Draft available upon request

Abstract

GDP measures do not incorporate all intangible investment undertaken by firms, although the importance of intangible assets has been increasingly recognized. To provide a more comprehensive picture of macroeconomic conditions, this paper measures intangible investment in Japan, including additional intangible assets that are currently under discussion for inclusion in national accounts. The findings show that intangible investment in Japan is more than twice as large as reported in current national accounts statistics, and that its ratio to GDP is increasing and exceeds that of the United States. Incorporating these additional intangible assets has significant implications for macroeconomic measurement. First, Japan’s five-year GDP growth rate is estimated to increase by approximately 1 percentage point relative to the current measure, while the corresponding estimate for the United States remains largely unchanged. In addition, the impact on GDP varies substantially across industries. Finally, the estimated labor share decreases by about 4 percentage points in both countries.
